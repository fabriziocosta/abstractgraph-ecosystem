\begin{abstract}
What makes one graph representation distinguish two graphs that another
collapses? The answer often lies in the structures each representation chooses
to expose: neighborhoods reveal one kind of difference, paths another, and
cycles yet another. Comparing such choices is difficult when every method has
its own representation and computational machinery. Abstract Graphs place
these choices in a common space. An interpretation graph records structures in
the base graph, and every operator maps one or more Abstract Graphs to another
Abstract Graph. We can therefore compose structural views without giving up
their connection to the original nodes and edges.  We study when these changes separate controlled graph pairs, whether
the resulting distinctions are accessible to simple predictive models, and
how much time, memory, and representation size they require. This also lets us
separate structural collapse from information lost during pooling or bounded
hashing. The resulting question is not which representation is universally
best, but which structural distinctions are worth their computational cost.
\end{abstract}
