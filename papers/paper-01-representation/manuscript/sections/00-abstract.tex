\begin{abstract}
What makes one graph representation distinguish two graphs that another
collapses? The answer often lies in the structures each representation chooses
to expose: neighborhoods reveal one kind of difference, paths another, and
cycles yet another. Comparing these choices is difficult when every method has
its own representation and computational machinery. Abstract Graphs place them
in a common mapped, graph-valued space. An interpretation graph records
structures in the base graph, so structural views can be composed while
remaining connected to the nodes and edges that produced them. We test whether
established representations can be reproduced faithfully, whether graph-valued
composition changes discrimination beyond simply concatenating component
feature vectors, and whether the responsible structures can be localized in
the base graph. We measure these effects on predeclared, held-out structural
contrasts and compare them with runtime, memory, and representation size. The
aim is not a universal expressivity ranking, but a controlled account of which
distinctions an expression exposes, where they arise, and what they cost.
\end{abstract}
