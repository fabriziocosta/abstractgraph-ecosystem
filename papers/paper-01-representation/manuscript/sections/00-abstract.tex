\begin{abstract}
Graph kernels differ principally in the structures they turn into features:
labels, walks, paths, graphlets, refined neighborhoods, or relations between
rooted subgraphs. Implementing each family as a separate method obscures both
their common construction and the feature families obtained by combining them.
Abstract Graphs provide a mapped, graph-valued language for this purpose. An
interpretation graph records derived structures while retaining their source
nodes and edges, and operators transform this representation compositionally.
We audit whether prominent graph-kernel feature maps have faithful Abstract
Graph programs, bounded proxies, or require operations not currently present.
We then use additive, ordered, and relational composition to construct
higher-order mapped features. Independent parity tests establish faithful
correspondences; controlled graph families describe the equivalence classes
induced by selected programs; and scaling experiments measure runtime, memory,
and representation size. The contribution is a compositional language and an
explicit account of its coverage and computational boundaries, not a claim
that a program discovers structures other than those it was defined to encode.
\end{abstract}
