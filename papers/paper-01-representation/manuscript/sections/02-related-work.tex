\section{Structural Views in Existing Graph Representations}
\label{sec:related-work}

The idea of representing a graph through its parts has a long history. Graph
kernels commonly decompose two graphs into substructures, compare those
substructures, and aggregate the comparisons. The Weisfeiler--Lehman subtree
kernel obtains features from successive neighborhood relabellings
\cite{Shervashidze2011WeisfeilerLehman}; NSPDK instead counts pairs of rooted
neighborhoods at prescribed radii and distances \cite{Costa2010NSPDK}. Both
methods make a structural choice before learning begins. They differ in what
they expose, but they share the progression from decomposition to identity and
aggregation that we use in this paper. Abstract Graphs retain the decomposition
as a graph-valued object rather than proceeding directly to a kernel value or
feature vector.

Subgraph GNNs revisit the same choice in a neural setting. ESAN represents a
graph as a collection of subgraphs selected by a policy and shows that this
policy affects expressive power \cite{Bevilacqua2022ESAN}. Later analyses
relate broad families of subgraph GNNs through their symmetries
\cite{Frasca2022SubgraphSymmetries} and organize them into expressivity classes
using subgraph Weisfeiler--Lehman tests \cite{Zhang2023SubgraphWL}. These works
are the closest contemporary comparison to ours: they make subgraph selection
a design choice rather than an incidental preprocessing step. Their primary
object, however, is a neural architecture over a particular family of
subgraph-selection policies. Our primary object is the structural expression
itself. It may later feed a kernel, a linear model, or a neural model, and its
mapped components and relations remain available for inspection.

This literature also shows why discrimination should not be summarized as a
single notion of ``more expressive.'' The ability to count a structure depends
on both the structure and the representation \cite{Chen2020CountSubstructures}.
Path-based methods, for example, distinguish some graph families that other
expressive architectures collapse, yet the converse can also hold
\cite{Graziani2024PathExpressivity}. Such incomparability supports our decision
to report the graph pairs separated by each expression rather than assume that
operators form a universal refinement hierarchy.

Greater discrimination usually has a price. Subgraph-aware WL seeks
subgraph-level distinctions without the cost of running a model independently
on every subgraph \cite{Wang2022SubgraphAwareWL}; more recent work selects a
smaller collection of subgraphs to balance expressivity and computation
\cite{Southern2025WalkCentrality}. These studies motivate our cost measurements,
but our comparison differs in two respects. We include non-neural structural
expressions, and we treat runtime, memory, and representation size as separate
objectives. This makes a Pareto frontier more informative than a single ordered
expressivity scale.

Finally, the word \emph{algebra} connects this work to research on graph-valued
operations. Universal graph algebra studies how graph operations give rise to
derived operations through parallel, instantiated, and sequential composition
\cite{Wolter2023GraphAlgebras}. Compositional property-graph languages likewise
seek graph-to-graph closure so that the result of one query can become the
input to another \cite{Arenas2026CompositionalGraphs}. These efforts establish
that graph-valued closure is a broader mathematical and language-design idea.
Here it serves a different question: how does composing structural
interpretations change the distinctions available to a learning system?

Our contribution therefore does not rest on decomposition, graph operations,
or expressivity analysis in isolation. It lies in bringing them together in a
common intermediate representation. Heterogeneous operators remain closed over
Abstract Graphs, interpretation nodes retain their source mappings, established
representations can be written as expressions in the same language, and their
effects can be compared under one discrimination--accessibility--cost protocol.
