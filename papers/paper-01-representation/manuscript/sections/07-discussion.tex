\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The principal distinction of this work is between closure and discrimination.
Closure says that a well-typed expression remains in the AG space. It gives a
stable substrate for composition, serialization, inspection, and extension.
It does not say that operators progressively refine a representation. A cycle
operator may expose a distinction collapsed by neighborhoods; an aggregation
or filter may remove one exposed earlier; a valid composite may simply encode
a different structural view. The empirical partitions and Pareto frontiers
make these consequences observable without turning them into algebraic axioms.

Mapped structure also makes comparisons more diagnostic. When two signatures
differ, the responsible interpretation nodes can be traced to concrete base
subgraphs. Conversely, identity width can collapse distinct certificates even
when the operator expression retained them. Separating operator, certificate,
hash, aggregation, and model effects is therefore essential.

The minimal decomposition interface is important for adoption. Domain experts
can contribute a structural grouping without implementing the surrounding
representation machinery, and independently developed operators remain
composable. The same property creates an opportunity for automatically
generated operators. It does not make arbitrary generated code trustworthy:
contracts, property tests, sandboxing, and semantic review remain necessary,
especially when a callback is stochastic, expensive, label-dependent, or
returns malformed groups.

Threats to validity include the selected graph families, canonicalization and
attribute equivalence, imperfect baseline reproduction, probe sensitivity,
and hardware-dependent costs. Synthetic controls establish mechanism but not
real-world usefulness; real datasets add ecological validity but rarely reveal
the exact structural distinction responsible for performance. Competitive
predictive learning and learned explanations remain subjects of later work.
